**BoilerFlowControl logic and physics review — 13 September 2026**

Reviewed local revision `c3a80a6`, including the specification, core equations, coordinator, state persistence, configuration and tests, plus the adjacent OT integration's radiator survey and DHW gating. This is a review; the integration and live boiler settings have not been changed. No live telemetry was collected. Boiler model/minimum useful output, cylinder/coil specification, and actual cylinder setpoint remain unverified.

**Conclusion:** retain weather compensation, bounded targets, the EMS setpoint heartbeat and separate heating/DHW regimes. Fix DHW completion and intervention bookkeeping first. Replace unconditional cycling-temperature reductions with evidence-based diagnosis. These offer concrete reliability and comfort benefits; incremental fuel savings require measurement.

The existing suite passes: **103 tests**. Nine additional reproductions also pass, asserting the problematic current behaviours rather than desired future behaviour. They are supplied alongside this review. Existing passing tests establish consistency with the programmed rules, not physical correctness.

**1. High priority: the DHW fallback can prevent the cylinder reaching its target.**

Evidence: `core/curve.py:216–245`, `core/model.py:99–108`, `coordinator.py:512–513` (all component paths are under `custom_components/boiler_flow_control/`).

After the second cycling episode, the sticky state forces the flow target to `dhw_flow_min`, default 55°C, across subsequent charges and restarts until manually reset. Reproduction: cylinder reading 60°C and holding state produce a 55°C flow target. A coil supplied continuously at 55°C cannot bring its surrounding water to 60°C. Actual overshoot, stratification or an independent immersion heater do not make that a dependable charging policy.

There is a separate route to the same problem: when DHW is demanded but the cylinder sensor is missing, the coordinator uses the heating target. At 15°C outdoors, the reproduced DHW flow target is just 35°C.

Recommendation: read the configured cylinder target and preserve its ability to complete a charge, including any existing hygiene cycle. A cycling fault should disable the experimental optimisation and use a commissioned DHW fallback within boiler/cylinder limits, with an alert. Add charge duration and temperature-progress monitoring. Do not substitute another universal temperature without checking the cylinder and boiler specifications. Near the end of a charge, completion takes precedence over minimising return temperature. HSE's temperature-control guidance uses storage at least at 60°C; a 55°C primary-flow hold is incompatible with achieving that using the coil alone. [HSE guidance](https://www.hse.gov.uk/legionnaires/hot-and-cold.htm)

Practical benefit: reliable hot water, fewer prolonged unsuccessful calls, and preservation of the intended cylinder temperature regime. High confidence in the defect; its occurrence depends on the live configuration and whether the hold is reached.

**2. High priority: lowering flow is not a universal remedy for cycling.**

Evidence: `core/curve.py:147–156` and `188–245`. Heating cycling produces −3 K; DHW cycling produces −5 K then the 55°C hold. Burner power is read and discarded at `coordinator.py:403`; current flow is recorded but does not inform the control action.

The relevant energy balance is approximately:

`C_effective × dT_water/dt = P_boiler − Q_emitters − Q_pipe_losses`.

For sustained operation, connected emitters must absorb approximately the boiler's minimum useful output. If they cannot, surplus heat raises water temperature until the boiler shuts down. Lowering water temperature normally reduces emitter output at unchanged valve openings and water flow. It can therefore make minimum-output cycling worse. A higher temperature can sometimes lengthen a burn, but can also increase return temperature and room overshoot: simply reversing the sign is not a universal fix either. This reasoning follows the hydronic energy balance described by [Caleffi](https://www.caleffi.com/en-us/blog/ask-caleffi-thermal-equilibrium).

For a cylinder coil, a useful approximation is `Q_coil ≈ UA × ΔT_log_mean`. With similar flow and local cylinder temperature, lowering primary temperature reduces coil heat transfer. A high return alone does not prove that the coil is “saturated”: heat transport is `Q = mass_flow × specific_heat × (flow − return)`. A small water-side temperature drop at a large flow rate can still transport substantial power. Flow restrictions can themselves cause short cycling. [Caleffi heat-exchanger applications](https://www.caleffi.com/en-us/blog/6-heat-exchanger-applications)

Recommendation: initially make cycling detection diagnostic, then permit bounded trials only after identifying the cause. Log flame/burner state, burn duration, actual flow versus target, return, burner modulation and the appliance relay's call-for-heat state. Distinguish relay-requested stops from boiler temperature-limit stops, startup overshoot from minimum-output saturation, and heating starts from DHW starts. Treat the configured minimum-power percentage as a control parameter, not evidence that the appliance can modulate to zero kW. The model's actual minimum output matters.

Validate the ignition source against the boiler's burner-start counter or flame state. EMS-ESP documents `heating_active` as a calculated burner/valve/pump signal; its suitability should be checked on this installation. Its documentation also distinguishes estimated energy derived from modulation from measured fuel use. [EMS-ESP special functions](https://docs.emsesp.org/Special-Functions)

Practical benefit: avoids counterproductive interventions and directs attention to pump flow, bypasses, emitter availability or minimum output when those are the real limitation. The achievable reduction in starts remains unquantified.

**3. High priority: cycling attempts can fail before their target has been applied.**

Evidence: `coordinator.py:495–510`, `523–545`; `core/policy.py:183–195`.

The DHW guard updates and persists its attempt counter before hysteresis and the service call are evaluated. Reproduced sequence: send 70°C; detect three starts; count a 65°C intervention but re-send 70°C because the target's ten-minute hold has not expired; detect three further starts; enter sticky hold, still without ever having sent 65°C. The same persistence condition allows attempts during manual hold and after service failure. An unchanged exempt target also resets `last_target_change`, although its value did not change.

Recommendation: separate proposed, sent and confirmed/applied targets. Start the intervention evaluation window only after that intervention has been applied; allow thermal settling and a meaningful observation period. Do not count attempts while the controller is overridden, while a write failed, or while hysteresis retained another target. Only advance `last_target_change` when the effective value changes.

The first intervention of a new DHW charge also counts heating ignitions from the preceding ten minutes: a reproduction enters DHW with an immediate intervention based entirely on preceding heating starts. Scope events to the current charge and classify their operating context. Persist `correction_k` if resuming a charge after restart; `hub.py:69–92` currently persists attempts/timestamps/hold but omits that correction.

Practical benefit: prevents false fault declarations and unnecessary low-temperature holds. High confidence, directly reproduced.

**4. High priority: entering DHW does not bypass the heating hold.**

Evidence: `coordinator.py:523–533`. Leaving DHW is exempt from hysteresis; entering is not, unless a separate return condition happens to exempt it.

Reproduction: heating just wrote 55°C, DHW requests 70°C, and the next call still writes 55°C. A recently changed heating or idle target can therefore delay the required DHW target for the remaining ten-minute hold. With a lower heating curve this may provide almost no cylinder heating.

Recommendation: make both transitions immediate on a confirmed operating-mode change, while retaining appropriate smoothing within each regime. A confirmed DHW request can also trigger a refresh rather than waiting for the next minute poll. Avoid letting the heating return ceiling govern DHW hysteresis: currently `return_over_ceiling` uses the heating ceiling even in DHW.

Practical benefit: shorter and more predictable cylinder recovery. High confidence, directly reproduced.

**5. Medium priority: the return ceiling is too aggressive and can retain an obsolete penalty.**

Evidence: `core/curve.py:126–139`, `coordinator.py:475–484`, `core/model.py:67–72`.

The controller subtracts 2 K per refresh, to −20 K, while return exceeds 50°C. It adds 2 K back when below, without a deadband, flow-stability gate or elapsed-time scaling. Refreshes caused by user controls can accelerate it. A stale/missing return freezes the existing penalty indefinitely; the reproduction retains −20 K. Heating/DHW separation helps, but the accumulator still runs in idle/off/manual-hold modes and sees residual DHW heat immediately after DHW ends.

Recommendation: treat return temperature as an efficiency objective constrained by comfort, not an absolute safety ceiling. Use sensor calibration, filtering, a deadband, elapsed-time-based correction and saturation handling. Adapt only during established heating circulation after settling. Expire or gradually remove unsupported correction when return data becomes stale. Do not allow old penalties to dominate renewed high room demand.

A condensing gas boiler benefits from return temperatures around/below its flue-gas dew point, approximately 55°C; 50°C is an efficiency preference rather than a universal hard boundary. [Viessmann explanation](https://www.viessmann.co.uk/en/heating-advice/boilers/what-temperature-to-set-condensing-boiler.html)

Practical benefit: less temperature hunting and less underheating after DHW or sensor problems. Exact deadband and rates need the strap-on sensors' observed lag and noise.

**6. Medium priority: demand and mode inference need confidence and room context.**

Evidence: `core/policy.py:52–82`, `coordinator.py:415–447`, `core/curve.py:72–84`.

Using maximum zone demand protects a demanding room, but does not estimate total thermal load. A small radiator at 100% and many large radiators at 100% give the same signal; using it to decide whether the boiler's minimum output is excessive is weak. Preserve worst-room demand for comfort, and use a separate estimate of connected heat absorption for cycling analysis. Add room temperature error and its trend before raising the whole system: a persistent demand can also reflect an undersized emitter or open window.

DHW inference treats `[0, unavailable]` as “all zones zero” when the aggregate is 100. That was reproduced, and an existing test explicitly expects it. Missing or stale zones could instead conceal real heating demand, producing an inappropriate DHW target. Require sufficient fresh evidence, debounce/latch the inferred state, and expose its confidence. Prioritise reliable cylinder-controller/valve evidence where available. Cylinder, outdoor and demand inputs currently lack the return sensor's age checks; reject non-finite values and normalise units too.

A smaller timer defect also exists: when correction is already zero, `active=False` returns without clearing the high/low sustain timestamps. Reproduction includes an inactive interval in the subsequent “sustained demand” period. Clear those timers whenever accumulation is inactive.

The adjacent OT integration still gates its live flow reading using its configured DHW signal (`ot_thermostat_control/hub.py:84–95`). If that is the unreliable raw relay, an inferred DHW charge can still contaminate its radiator calculation. Share a consistent DHW-active state, distinguishing DHW-only from mixed operation and actual radiator circulation.

Practical benefit: fewer incorrect mode switches and better room comfort, especially under RF dropouts. Room feedback offers more value than replacing the curve with a complicated model immediately.

**7. Medium priority: apply limits before bookkeeping and make shadow mode representative.**

Evidence: `coordinator.py:277–285`, `535–545`; `core/policy.py:174–178`.

The service call caps the value to the live max-flow number, but memory and reported target retain the uncapped decision. Reproduction: send 50°C while remembering 55°C; later raise the dial to 70°C and the unchanged live 50°C falsely triggers manual hold. Resolve the effective target first, including entity limits and supported step, and use that same value for transmission, memory and diagnostics. If a configured max-flow source is unavailable, expose a deliberate fallback policy. A successful HA service handler is also not by itself confirmation that the boiler adopted the value; account for delayed readback and quantisation.

Shadow `would_write` returns the raw target before hold/hysteresis/max-flow arbitration, and does not advance an independent DHW trial state. It is consequently not a faithful simulation of auto operation. Use separate shadow state with the same decision path, without contaminating live memory. Clearly label requested, effective, sent and read-back values; report the DHW corrections actually used, rather than only the separately computed heating corrections.

Practical benefit: trustworthy commissioning and fewer false manual holds. High confidence in the capped-memory discrepancy, directly reproduced.

**8. Lower priority: refine the weather curve only after calibration.**

Evidence: `core/curve.py:35–45`. The inverse radiator exponent is a reasonable starting point: building heat loss roughly follows indoor/outdoor difference, while radiator output increases nonlinearly with mean water-to-room temperature. Applying this relationship to flow temperature is an approximation whose accuracy depends on the changing flow/return drop. It is not inherently a reason to replace the curve.

Current defaults produce:

| Outdoor temperature | Heating curve |
|---|---:|
| −3°C | 55.0°C |
| 0°C | 51.4°C |
| 5°C | 45.2°C |
| 10°C | 38.4°C |
| 15°C | 35.0°C |

A more physical radiator model is `Q_rad ≈ Q_DT50 × (((T_flow + T_return)/2 − T_room)/50)^n`, with a manufacturer-specific exponent when available. Delta-T radiator ratings refer to water temperature relative to the room; Stelrad publishes correction factors consistent with roughly `n = 1.3`. [Rating convention](https://www.stelrad.com/advice-hub/glossary/), [correction factors](https://www.stelradprofessional.com/stelrad-correction-factor/)

Using the survey's approximate Studio output of 1,242 W at ΔT50, assuming 20°C room air and a 10 K flow/return drop, calculated output is about 504 W at 50/40°C and 377 W at 45/35°C: a 25% reduction from only a 5 K flow reduction. This is an illustrative calculation, not a measured output or proof of the room's heat requirement. The existing “50°C gives 41% output” statement depends on that return/room assumption.

First calibrate the curve against the weakest occupied room and provide a bounded warm-up response to persistent room error. The ten-minute demand filter plus twenty-minute sustain requirement can otherwise delay the first increase by over half an hour after a step from zero demand. Consider per-zone heat-loss/emitter limits when measured room behaviour supports them. Do not equate demand percentage directly with radiator watts.

**Recommended implementation and validation order**

1. Correct DHW completion/fallback, transition timing, effective-target memory and applied-intervention bookkeeping. Replace the permanent low-temperature fault action.
2. Add trustworthy telemetry and diagnostic cycling classification; qualify sensors and mode inference. Repair/calibrate the existing return/flow sensors before relying on them.
3. Introduce slow, bounded return optimisation and room-error feedback. Calibrate before adding detailed adaptive models.
4. Compare matched operation with and without each optimisation. For heating, record gas use alongside outdoor conditions, schedules and room temperature error. For DHW, compare charges with similar starting temperatures, target, and draw-off conditions. Track charge completion/time, starts per charge, burn lengths, heating starts per burner-hour, and gas per comparable delivered service. Collect burner/relay transitions event-wise and temperatures at their native reporting rate; minute averages alone can hide short burns.

The baseline matters: the specification says heating was already around 50°C flow. If return was already condensing, incremental savings may be modest, and fewer starts alone do not prove lower gas consumption. For illustration only, raising seasonal efficiency from 92% to 94% would reduce fuel for identical delivered heat by `1 − 0.92/0.94 ≈ 2.1%`; that is arithmetic, not a forecast for this house. The immediate justified benefits are reliability, hot-water recovery and avoidance of counterproductive control actions.
